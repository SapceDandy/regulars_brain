# Regulars: events, digital growth, and an AI-operated member experience

Research and proposed design · October 13, 2026 · Central Texas

## Recommendation

Lead the initial owner pitch with events and digital growth: Regulars plans and promotes selected events, coordinates guest attendance and seating, and operates the digital member experience for $500/month. Include a bounded local-search and digital-support service within that fee. The broader company remains an AI system attached to a physical venue, connecting guests, venues, partners, and eventually operations. Lockers provide the physical launch offering and a concrete member relationship. Food perks are removed from the membership foundation. The proposed $49/month founding club and repriced locker bundles remain price tests; the mature storage scenario remains 48 physical lockers with separately measured seating capacity.

The customer proposition is: “I can plan my night, secure a good place for my party, and arrive somewhere people know me.” The owner proposition is: “For $500/month, we plan and promote selected events, help guests book and attend, run the Regulars digital membership and seating tools, maintain key discovery information, and report the results.” The intended outcome is additional contribution and less repetitive coordination. It remains a pilot hypothesis, not demonstrated willingness to pay or a guarantee of attendance or revenue.

The strongest finding is that access is a specific entitlement. Across the examples below, that entitlement can mean permission to enter a private space, an earlier booking window, access to reserved inventory, a confirmed seat, guest-hosting privileges, or a service contact. Those benefits have different capacity requirements. The proposed Regulars contract must specify which ones a member buys.

## Owner-facing $500/month events and digital support package

Position the founder's digital work as included service with a defined scope. Its purpose is to help the venue earn through events, groups, memberships, bottles, and repeat visits. The shared software and reusable workflow must make delivery repeatable. The earlier $500 venue fee includes this proposed scope; a second agency fee is not added here. Physical installation retains its separately quoted target below.

### What discovery work actually means

Google says complete, accurate profile information can help local visibility, and provides event/update posts on Search and Maps. Its post policy allows regulated businesses to post but restricts content about regulated products, including alcohol. Keep Google posts focused on appropriate venue activities and event details. Posting is a customer-information channel; this offer does not claim that a particular posting frequency guarantees higher rankings. [S28–S29]

For AI discovery, maintain clear, crawlable, useful venue information and current business details. Google emphasizes foundational SEO and original helpful content; its generative-search inclusion is not guaranteed. Bing describes accurate local details, clear content, and fresh information as useful to AI experiences. These support a practical maintenance service rather than a guaranteed recommendation in every AI assistant. [S30–S31]

For eligible public events, create a page for the individual event with accurate dates and location, and appropriate event markup when the publishing system supports it. Google's event feature excludes events requiring membership or invitation before attendance, so a private member gathering must not be represented as an eligible public event for that feature. It may still have a useful information/RSVP page. [S32]

Google provides review links and QR codes and supports owner responses. This proposed workflow asks for honest feedback without payment or favorable-review filtering. Profile metrics such as directions and website clicks are useful indicators, but they are not verified event attendance or incremental sales. Measure actual attendance through the guest workflow and use venue sales data when available. [S33–S34]

### Delivery economics and founder workload

Target approximately four to six hours of initial digital onboarding per venue, after the shared product exists and assuming usable account access/media. Target two to four recurring hours monthly per venue after templates and integrations are established. These are planning budgets to validate with time logs; complex legacy websites, guest exceptions, or fresh software development can exceed them. Shared product development and physical installation labor are additional work, not hidden within that recurring estimate.

At ten venues, the fee produces $5,000 monthly gross company revenue and the recurring service target consumes roughly 20–40 founder hours. Tool costs, payment costs, sales, development, and support reduce the proceeds. The package becomes viable through standardization and controlled exceptions, not through treating the founder's time as costless.

Use one approved event record as the source for the event page, social assets, suitable profile update, email invitation, reminders, screen notices, and results report. AI creates/checks channel-specific drafts and coordinates the configured workflow. Record facts such as date, place, capacity, booking terms, media rights, and the host once, then reuse them. Owner-approved plans and reliable booking/event data govern publication and seat commitments. A single knowledge base prevents different channels from announcing inconsistent details.

Illustrative fee break-even only: at a 50% incremental contribution margin, $1,000 in genuinely additional spending produces $500 contribution before the platform fee. Forty additional $25 visits would meet that arithmetic threshold. Additional event expenses outside that margin must also be covered. At $3,000 added spending, the same assumption yields $1,500 contribution and $1,000 after the fee, before any additional excluded event expense and general overhead. Existing visitors merely changing the time of their visit are not automatically additional.

The events-led offer gives the founder an immediate service reason to access the venue's calendar, approved communications, guest workflow, and reporting. Standardizing that work into the product is how the first digital services support the broader AI system. Membership and locker prices below are unchanged by this revision; the existing contribution forecasts remain unvalidated.

## The initial offering and proposed prices

Offer the owner a twelve-locker installation plus a limited member club and an AI coordination pilot. Retain the $500/month venue fee and the $2,500 total installation target, including founder compensation. The physical build still requires a safe, quoted, repeatable design and a site check before a fixed installation promise; this research does not approve the earlier IKEA hanging retrofit or its load capacity. Bottle purchases are separately priced. No additional percentage of membership or bottle sales is proposed for the pilot.

Within the events-and-digital-service package, the product should deliver five connected functions:

1. **Membership and locker management:** a guest pass, paid member status, billing/renewal tracking, and whole/shared locker allocations with authorized bottle-service records. There are four named shared positions per physical locker; storage capacity and membership count remain distinct.
2. **Seat and guest coordination:** the venue's approved inventory, booking requests, confirmations, waitlists, reconfirmations, and a staff arrival/guest list. AI communicates and coordinates within the actual inventory rules.
3. **An AI guest coordinator:** help guests plan visits, remember voluntarily supplied preferences, arrange available seats or group requests, send relevant consented invitations, and answer from venue-approved information. Record exceptions and hand them to the designated staff contact.
4. **One game-night experience:** approved display messages, event reminders, host cues, and permitted entertainment scheduling using suitable event data and existing authorized equipment. The owner sets the permitted actions and schedule. The rollout must demonstrate reliable execution before expanding automation to more equipment or programs.
5. **A club routine and owner report:** two recurring small groups and a named host, with an automated weekly snapshot where supported and one monthly review connecting attendance, renewal, additional contribution, and coordination effort. The AI can suggest group invitations and programming; staff perform the welcome and physical service.

These are build-and-deliver requirements for the proposed pilot, not a claim that every feature is already implemented. Initial service should specify its response hours and the staff responsibilities. As workflows prove reliable, automate their routine steps. A human performing the whole process indefinitely would leave the labor economics unresolved.

### Why a free pass and a limited paid club coexist

The free pass lets more guests participate in the venue's digital experience and creates a route into membership. It can support approved shopping rewards, public game participation, relevant notifications, and partner referrals. The paid club sells actual access and service. Exclusivity comes from limited seat and hosting capacity and consistent member treatment. Establish respectful, transparent membership and guest rules; a waiting list should reflect genuine capacity limits.

Begin with a proposed founding cohort of roughly 20–30 named paying members in total, including locker members, only if inventory and hosting capacity support it. This is a pilot design ceiling to verify, not a forecast or a mandatory quota. A fully occupied half-whole/half-shared twelve-locker bank already has 30 named locker members; adding ordinary memberships requires additional access and service capacity. Separate the guaranteed-seat inventory from the booking-priority pool.

Premium pricing has local precedents, but the facilities matter. Pershing advertises $150/month for its under-30 tier, excluding its monthly food/beverage credit, alongside substantial private facilities and programming. The Shop Houston advertises $200/month with lounges, simulators, automotive facilities and events. Their offers do not establish willingness to pay $200 at Third Base or Finley's. [S5, S27]

Those are gross membership receipts before delivery costs, tax treatment, processing, refunds, displaced existing contribution, and the venue fee. One hundred premium customers is a growth hypothesis, not the initial forecast.

During Month 1, establish the venue's rules, clean up the agreed discovery information, implement the pass and booking/locker workflows, deliver the first event promotion and defined AI coordination service. During Month 2, repeat the campaign workflow and test one approved game-night automation and recurring host program. During Month 3, add one agreed sponsor or partner referral workflow if the core service fits its workload and is reliable. These are milestones to schedule, not claims of completed features or guaranteed signed partnerships. Affiliate cashback remains dependent on approved merchants and settled funding. Paid-points wagering contributes zero to the Texas base case.

This staged offer keeps guest membership understandable while accumulating the workflows, permissions, integrations, and outcome information required for the larger system connecting customers, venues, partners, and operations. Automated operations are a later product to validate. The immediate measures are successful confirmed-seat delivery, member renewal, genuinely additional contribution, and reduced routine coordination time without increased service failures.

## Research scope and evidence limits

This review covers 17 membership/access programs, including clubs, cultural venues, sports hospitality, and hospitality platforms. It draws on more than ten independent official domains, published operating rules, a current Austin membership-manager job description, and three primary academic publications. It does not treat advertised benefits as proof of customer satisfaction, profitability, or Regulars demand.

Large private clubs have assets and programming that a neighborhood bar does not automatically possess. Their prices establish the terms of their advertised offers; they are not price comparisons for the proposed bar pilot. Recommendations below are adaptations and inferences, clearly separated from operator descriptions. No venue has agreed to allocate Regulars seating or provide a private room through this research.

The Battery's society example comes from its official legacy material and publicly indexed official calendar. Some dynamic sites expose limited extractable text. This review uses that example to identify a community design pattern, and does not promise a current Battery membership entitlement. The Ned was researched but excluded from the evidence table because the accessible pages were too incomplete to verify its full package.

## Developing the value of seats first

The useful asset is a seat with a desirable view, at a wanted time, available through a dependable process. The customer may value avoiding an early arrival, protecting a date or group outing, and knowing the party will sit together. These are plausible Regulars use cases. Cornell's casual-dining seating survey found that respondents valued the control reservations offered over their schedules; its findings are directional evidence, not a current Texas sports-bar conversion forecast. [S23]

Start by observing the venue on several relevant busy and quiet nights. Record which seats customers want, actual wait times, party sizes, arrival patterns, how long people stay, and whether groups leave because they cannot sit together. Map screen sightlines, table configurations, and the service area. Interview existing regulars about their last unsuccessful visit. The venue's actual seating inconvenience establishes the case for charging.

Offer three clearly distinguished forms of access:

| Access product | Actual promise | Appropriate use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ordinary club booking access | Members see a protected bank of seats earlier and receive priority cancellation alerts. A confirmed booking protects the named seat or agreed equivalent for the stated period. | Proposed $49/month pilot, paired with service and social programming. |
| Event seat or group table | A specific seat/party allocation for a specified event and time, at an explicitly stated price and with any deposit terms shown. | Individual high-demand occasions; general game attendance continues under the venue's ordinary admission policy. |
| Season seat or recurring table | Particular inventory is assigned across a published set of dates. | A limited premium product after the venue proves demand and quotes its opportunity cost. |

These are proposed product boundaries. Use $49 as the ordinary founding test and validate the conditional $199 contracted-seat bundle against actual inventory and paid interest. Determine the final recurring-seat price from reserved dates, service costs, and foregone walk-in contribution. The $500/month venue platform fee remains unchanged. Any separate ticketed artist event requires its own terms; membership does not imply tickets to every event.

### Proposed booking rules for the first installation

1. Identify a small, venue-approved bank of good-view seats. Describe the seat or zone on the booking page.
2. Open the member window, for example seven days before the selected event. This is a pilot setting, not a researched industry standard.
3. Begin with one active peak-event booking per named member. Every guest occupies separately counted inventory. Set a visible party-size limit; larger groups use a hosted-table request.
4. Confirm the event, seat/zone, party size, arrival deadline, and covered period. Once confirmed, staff must protect that inventory. A busy televised game should have a session long enough to cover the published game period; extensions need a stated policy.
5. Send a reconfirmation request before the event. Cancellations trigger a short acceptance window for the next person on the waitlist.
6. Release unbooked inventory at a stated cutoff. A booking can be held until a published grace deadline, such as 15 minutes, with a way to notify the host of a delay.
7. Repeated no-shows lead to disclosed booking restrictions. Any deposit or fee needs clear terms and a genuine financial purpose. A refundable deposit is an obligation until earned or refunded; it is not membership income.
8. If the venue fails to honor a confirmed booking, offer an equivalent allocation the member accepts or an agreed service remedy. Record the failure and correct the process.

BFI provides a particularly clear reserved-inventory example. Barbican and Resy show why priority access must be distinguished from guaranteed availability. Soho House's FAQ also shows reconfirmation and waitlist procedures, and describes North American screening circumstances where even an RSVP is not a seating guarantee. Regulars should make its own confirmation promise unambiguous. [S2, S3, S20, S26]

The customer-facing value is the successful visit. Preferred entry helps when entry is genuinely a bottleneck; its value will be low at a venue with no queue. A selected viewing zone can feel special when it provides a good experience and proper hosting. A label on an ordinary chair alone is an untested value proposition.

### Seat capacity and the 48-locker program

Forty-eight lockers are storage inventory. In the earlier half-exclusive/half-shared mature mix, there are 120 named VIP accounts before ordinary memberships. Each seat entitlement must still fit the venue's peak-event inventory.

Illustration only: 16 member seats across four game sessions produce 64 seat-session allocations in a month. A member and guest at two sessions consume four allocations, so the arithmetic ceiling is 16 such packages. That monthly ceiling still cannot guarantee everyone access to the same popular session. A 20% allocation reserve would reduce the illustrative package count to 12 whole packages, but 20% is a proposed buffer, not an established benchmark.

For booking-access memberships, sell access to limited inventory with honest availability and measure how often members succeed. For guaranteed season seats, sell only the specific seat-session inventory the venue can deliver. Track unmet demand and publish relevant limitations before collecting recurring fees. Reserve inventory for ordinary members so VIP allocations cannot consume the entire ordinary membership benefit.

Locker VIP includes core club membership and adds storage/bottle management and a more hands-on hosting contact. A season-seat contract is a separately allocated upgrade. Do not attach an unlimited seat entitlement to every locker account. New seat capacity can come from another event session or approved area; buying more lockers does not create seats.

## Developing the social experience

The proposed social service is helping a member find a familiar group and making the first few visits easy. Soho House documents interest groups, messaging, and local House groups. Its current Austin membership-manager role includes onboarding, introductions, retention, and follow-up on problems. This shows the work behind the experience; it does not establish the staffing costs of our pilot. [S8–S9]

Hall's friendship studies found associations between shared time, leisure activity, and relationship closeness. They do not establish a guaranteed number of club visits needed to make a friend. Our inference is to build recurring small-group activity and welcoming introductions, then measure whether participants choose to return together. [S25]

### A specific member journey

| Stage | What Regulars and the host do | Member value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Join | Ask about preferred events, usual companions, and interest in meeting others. Let the member choose a private friends-group experience or a social table. | Participation fits the member's reason for joining. |
| First booking | Offer actual available seats and one suitable gathering. Give the member a named contact and clear arrival instructions. | The plan is easy to follow. |
| First arrival | Welcome the member by their preferred name. Explain the seat/service arrangement and, with permission, introduce two relevant people. | An approachable beginning rather than an awkward room full of strangers. |
| Weeks 2–4 | Invite the member back to the same group or nearby seats at a recurring activity. A host remembers previous introductions. | Familiarity develops over repeated visits. |
| Established member | Enable guest hosting, proposing an activity, or serving as a group captain with clear responsibilities. | A useful role and greater agency in the club. |

Start with two groups of approximately 6–10 willing participants, not a large directory with no activity. These group sizes are implementation suggestions, not a scientific optimum. Each group needs a named host, one recurring time or event series, enough seats, and a clear purpose. The host can be trained venue staff or a compensated Regulars operator; hosting labor must be budgeted. A member captain can help after the format works, with appropriate supervision.

### Proposed starting formats

- **Third Base:** one recurring team-watch crew built around an actual customer-supported team; one fortnightly locals table for guests who want to meet people. Keep existing friend parties together when requested. Use designated viewing seats, RSVP lists, introductions, and a shared schedule.
- **Finley's:** one bottle-enthusiast circle connected to the locker customers; one fortnightly Round Rock social table. A bartender can lead a short discussion within an approved, costed format. Any purchased drinks are separately priced and served normally; included alcohol and free tastings are not promised by this design.
- **Both:** one monthly member-hosted occasion or guest speaker, chosen from actual member interests. A local musician, broadcaster, coach, or community organizer is a prospect, not a confirmed partner. Publish included and separately paid elements.

A suggested first-month social sequence is a welcome gathering, a repeat gathering with the same participants, a bring-a-guest opportunity within capacity, and a member-proposed session. After that, retain formats that members actually rebook. Attendance by different people at every event does not by itself demonstrate relationships developing.

The digital experience supports group RSVPs, seat assignments, introductions requested through a host, private communications, and feedback. Member directories and attendance visibility are opt-in. Allow people to enjoy their existing group without joining a mixer. Maintain ordinary respectful conduct rules, guest accountability, and a clear way to report unwanted solicitation. The premium experience should feel consistent with each venue's existing character.

## Hosting and recognition as distinct benefits

Give members a reliable route to arrange a date, birthday, watch party, or client outing. Let the member identify guests in advance and communicate seating needs. Staff should know who is hosting, who has arrived, and what was confirmed. A named contact can help coordinate timing, accessibility needs, and permitted bottle service. Limit group size and response hours to what the operation can deliver.

Guest-hosting rules appear in Pershing, Cowboys Club, Casa Cipriani, Home House, and Soho House. Concierge/booking contacts appear in Ronnie Scott's, Foundation Room, Blackbird, and Resy. These examples support treating hospitality coordination as a recognizable benefit; the willingness to pay at Finley's and Third Base must be tested. [S4–S5, S13–S15, S18, S20–S21, S26]

Status can grow from being a dependable participant, a thoughtful host, or a helpful group captain. Introduce members personally and give opt-in recognition for their contribution. A founding designation can mark the real initial cohort. Protect access and service standards so recognition remains meaningful. Expensive decor, celebrity access, and aggressive exclusivity are not assumed pilot assets.

## How the venue earns from this approach

The model has four potential contributions: membership dues after delivery costs; additional visits/group purchases; upgraded seat or hosting products when demand supports them; and later sponsor value from a genuinely active community. Affiliate credits remain a funded return-visit mechanism, outside the membership foundation. Paid-points wagering is outside this membership design and its Texas base-case contribution remains zero.

Seats also have an opportunity cost. If the same customer would occupy the same seat and spend the same amount without membership, only net dues and any demonstrated spending change are additional. Holding empty tables too long, extending low-spend occupancy, or shifting a better-paying party can reduce profit. The manager should measure revenue and contribution across the full time seats are occupied. Cornell's seat-hour work supports accounting for both check-opening and check-closing times rather than treating every sale as a new capacity gain. [S24]

Use this planning equation:

**Added contribution = net membership dues + contribution from genuinely additional purchases + earned seat/hosting charges + retained sponsor income − extra service/program costs − displaced existing contribution.**

Count the $500 venue fee once across the combined program. Track refundable booking deposits, prepaid balances, and annual dues separately from earned monthly contribution. Food perk expenses from the previous proposed package are removed; hosting, booking administration, and program expenses take their place. Earlier annual venue projections require recalculation with actual seat limits and quoted staffing before being reused as a pitch for this offer.

## What to build and validate first

For the first twelve-locker installation, build a member pass, a limited seat/zone calendar, confirmations/reconfirmations, guest lists, cancellation/waitlist handling, an AI-assisted guest coordination workflow, a simple host dashboard, and two recurring groups. Use manual exception handling during validation and record the effort so routine steps can be standardized and automated. Test the bounded game-night workflow against venue-approved plans. Automated attendance identification, new sponsor hardware, and a large social network are later implementation choices.

Run a six-week access-and-hosting pilot after the venue allocates inventory. Invite existing regulars whose recent experiences show a real seating or group-planning problem. Test the proposed $49/month founding offer with clear entitlements and cancellation terms. Separately test paid interest in the $199 contracted-seat bundle when its calendar is defined. A small paid cohort and renewals provide more useful evidence than hypothetical interest alone. There is no researched conversion rate or guaranteed cohort size in this report.

Measure confirmed-seat delivery, timely arrival, wait time, booking-request success, repeated failures, seat contribution per hour, host minutes per member, repeated group attendance, guest return, voluntary renewals, and contribution compared with the likely baseline. Ask whether members would keep paying for access and service without a discount. For return-visit lift, use a holdout or staggered invitation test where feasible; notifications and check-ins are not proof of extra spending.

Before expanding to 24 or 48 lockers, require positive measured contribution, a paid waitlist for storage, reliable booking fulfillment, and seat/program capacity for any additional membership entitlements. A second bank of lockers can support storage expansion while the seat offer remains limited. Expansion should follow separately verified demand for each entitlement.
