Devon's Founder Transition Plan

Prepared September 21, 2026. This is a working plan for moving from employment to a business that can support $6,000–$8,000 a month of personal spending. It develops the earlier venture-studio plan and keeps Regulars and KUDJO as the long-term concepts. It does not assume either has a paying customer. Current prices and traction must be confirmed through actual customer commitments.

The main decision is to give one offer most of your operating attention for the next 90 days. That offer earns more time through payment, useful customer results, manageable delivery, and renewal. Other ideas remain available for later.

Assumptions: a roughly nine-to-five job, about 20 business hours a week, and an income target measured after an illustrative tax reserve. Move the clock times around your actual job and commute. If 20 hours is unrealistic, reduce the scope and extend the timeline. The customer counts, prices, time limits, conversion examples, and departure thresholds below are proposed operating choices, not proven benchmarks or forecasts.

The mindset to practice

Take responsibility for a small, valuable result that someone will pay for. Then make that result reliable and repeatable. Keep ambition long term and make the next action small enough to finish today.

Sara Blakely's account of building Spanx includes a fax-sales job, $5,000 of startup savings, prototypes, and persistent contact with manufacturers. The useful lesson is turning an observed problem into repeated action. Her path does not establish a typical probability of success. [Sara Blakely's Masters of Scale interview](https://mastersofscale.com/sara-blakely-how-to-find-your-big-idea/)

Daymond John's early FUBU work included maintaining a Red Lobster job while building the business. His example is relevant to funding a transition through employment. [Daymond John's career history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daymond_John)

Janice Bryant Howroyd describes transparency, accountability, and personal values as foundations of her leadership. That is useful for the question of becoming someone other people want to work with. [Janice Bryant Howroyd's own biography](https://askjbh.com/about/)

These are different starting points. Success stories are selected examples and cannot separate effort from timing, resources, relationships, and luck. Use specific practices as experiments in your circumstances.

Here are five changes to rehearse:

1. Change “I need to know whether I am a successful person” to “I need evidence about this offer.” A declined proposal is information about a particular buyer, problem, timing, price, or trust gap. Ask which one, record it, and choose a next action.
2. Change “I need to build the whole vision” to “I need one buyer to use and pay for one useful outcome.” A new feature needs a connection to a sale, retention, reliable delivery, or a repeated support problem.
3. Change “I need confidence before I ask” to “I can practice a respectful ask while uncertain.” Complete the ask, then review it. Treat sales like lifting: consistent repetitions, feedback, and recovery.
4. Change “I worked all evening” to “What moved?” Track qualified conversations, paid commitments, customer outcomes, collected cash, renewals, and delivery hours. Effort is necessary; it still needs feedback.
5. Change “I have to do everything” to “I have to make sure the important things get done.” At first you do much of the work. Later you specify, delegate, inspect, and improve it.

My read of our conversations is that you can quickly connect a single product to a much larger business system. The scheduling risk is giving several future businesses time before one offer has repeatable demand. Keep a parking list for new ideas. Review it on Sunday; an idea does not automatically become a task.

Your first offer

Start with the strongest accessible buyer, urgent problem, and deliverable you can competently complete. Your experience in software, AI workflows, marketing, and operations is a starting asset. It is not yet proof that a particular customer will pay a particular price.

The default route to test is one paid, tightly scoped implementation or managed service. It should serve the same kinds of businesses you want to understand for Regulars or KUDJO where practical. A separate service line earns a test only after owner interviews show a valuable problem.

For example, an event-capable venue may have inquiries and quotes falling through gaps. An initial project could organize inquiries in its existing tools, assign next actions, configure approved follow-up workflows, and produce a weekly owner report. The venue's staff handles urgent daytime decisions, availability, and bookings. Do not promise live coverage that overlaps your job. An ongoing fee requires ongoing work and value; a one-time installation may belong under project pricing.

One concrete price experiment for that service is a $2,500, thirty-day managed pilot for one location and one workflow, with up to eight setup hours and four hours of reporting, support, and agreed improvements. Show the price and limits before the owner commits. Test whether the work can actually be delivered in those hours. Discuss the potential $2,500 monthly continuation up front; use it only when continuing service and customer value justify it. Otherwise quote a fixed project and a proportionate maintenance plan. A small, low-impact automation does not become worth $2,500 a month because that is your income goal.

Before selling that pilot, use the owner's records to test the value case. For illustration, five genuinely additional completed events contributing $1,500 each would create $7,500 before your fee. That is a hypothetical calculation, not a promised outcome. Check inquiry volume, existing conversion, capacity, and attribution before assuming those events are available to win. If the numbers cannot support the fee, choose a different scope or customer.

Regulars is the other immediate candidate if you can reach an owner with existing lockers, an operating program, and willingness to pay for member administration and measurement. Keep the locker requirement. Begin with member and locker records, staff check-in, service records, and the monthly owner review. Existing tools and a simple reliable workflow should carry the first pilot. Presence detection, cameras, and new infrastructure require later evidence.

Keep KUDJO centered on measurable acquisition, reactivation, and customer value. Current positioning should not reduce it to a discount product. Use whatever existing product functionality can reliably support a buyer's paid objective; do not presume old code or billing works today.

Use the first ten qualified owner conversations to choose the active offer. Give it at least 70 percent of business effort. Launch one new pilot at a time. Avoid spreading those ten conversations across ten unrelated industries.

For each candidate offer, answer six questions:

1. Can I reach the buyer this week?
2. Can the buyer show me a recent instance of the problem?
3. Is the loss or workload large enough to justify paying?
4. Can I deliver a useful result with my present skills and available hours?
5. Will I have access to the records needed to verify the result?
6. Does a paid pilot create knowledge or reusable work for the next similar buyer?

This cash model does not deduct a separate replacement salary for your own delivery work; assess that additional cost when judging whether the company can operate without you.

My starting preference is a narrow paid service or a ready-to-buy Regulars implementation, whichever has the clearest actual demand. The service can fund learning and reusable software. Converting to software is a later choice supported by several customers needing the same workflow.

Capacity comes before the client-count target

An earlier broad-service proposal allowed 32 hours per client each month. Six such clients require 192 delivery hours a month before selling or administration. That does not fit this plan.

A possible operating design is four ongoing founder hours per client per month after onboarding, with setup separately scoped and measured. Seven clients would then require 28 monthly delivery hours. That uses most of a seven-hour weekly delivery allocation, leaving little room for new implementations. Stagger starts, increase capacity through paid help, raise the price for greater scope, or reduce the number of clients. Do not assume automation achieves these hours until you have measured them.

If a $2,500 service requires substantial daily human attention, price and staff it accordingly. If the customer's benefit is too small to support the fee, a stronger closing script will not repair the economics.

Stage one: the first fourteen days

The work-ethic change is initiating external conversations before feeling completely ready. The evidence target is ten qualified owner conversations and at least one explicit paid-pilot proposal. A paid pilot is a target, not a promised two-week outcome.

Day one: record your available weekly hours, actual personal spending, existing tools, existing business arrangements, and any confirmed customer payments. Check the outside-work terms that apply to your job. Use personal equipment, accounts, and time. List ten reachable owners or people who could introduce you to them. Request the first five conversations.

Day two: add another ten prospects from the same buyer category. Practice the opening question and do any booked calls. Record what happened recently, how the owner deals with it, and why it matters. Do not lead with a product demonstration.

Day three: continue calls and appropriate follow-ups. Write a one-sentence problem statement using the owner's language. Review a real or redacted example of the workflow when available. Remove assumptions that interviews contradict.

Day four: describe one small paid pilot: one buyer, one problem, one result to measure, specific deliverables, client responsibilities, a start date, price, and limits. Prepare a short demonstration with sample data only when it helps the owner make a decision.

Day five: make a clear offer to a qualified owner. Send the agreed scope and invoice when the owner wants to proceed. Record objections and reasons for declining. Review the week and choose one improvement.

Week two: continue conversations, improve the same offer, and seek payment. Before starting delivery, settle scope, access, payment, and responsibilities. For a live Regulars program, the venue-specific operating requirements from the earlier plan still apply. Do not describe the pilot as live before the required pieces are in place.

A useful stop-and-review rule: after twenty substantive conversations with appropriate decision makers and no one willing to buy a relevant pilot, pause speculative development. Review the problem, buyer, value, proof, timing, and price with a mentor. This is a management checkpoint, not a statistical finding that the business is impossible.

Your weekday routine while employed

Assume a nine-to-five job. The following is a template. Keep the sequence if you move the times. Protect a sleep window; this example uses 10:30 p.m. to 6:30 a.m.

6:30–6:40 a.m.: get up, use the bathroom, get water, and open the plan prepared the previous evening. Keep social feeds closed. Read the one business outcome you intend to advance today. Write the next physical action, such as opening the prospect list and drafting the follow-up to a named owner.

6:40–6:50: practice one sales question or objection out loud. Use a peer or an AI role-play if helpful. Ten minutes is enough. The purpose is to rehearse the actual conversation you will have.

6:50–7:10: complete up to five relevant follow-ups. By the first thirty minutes of the day, finish the first one. Schedule written outreach for appropriate business hours. Follow up only where it is welcome and relevant.

7:10–7:35: prepare up to five researched first contacts from the list built on the weekend. Each identifies a relevant reason for contacting that business and makes one simple request. Avoid invented observations or claims of results you have not achieved.

7:35–7:40: record what you did and the next action for each live opportunity. Stop at the end of the block.

7:40–9:00: breakfast, preparation, movement if time allows, and commute. Move this window earlier or later around your actual travel requirements.

9:00–5:00: perform your job. On Tuesday and Thursday, a personal lunch break can hold one scheduled thirty-minute owner call if that is permitted and practical. Otherwise substitute an evening call slot or ask about another time. Do not double-book employment obligations.

5:00–7:00: commute, dinner, exercise or a walk, and a reset. Adjust the order to suit your actual day.

7:00–8:30 on Monday and Wednesday: deliver the current paid work. Before a sale, use this time for interview synthesis or the smallest demonstration needed for an active proposal. Keep broad product development out of the block unless it resolves the current business obstacle.

7:00–8:30 on Tuesday and Thursday: booked discovery calls, demonstrations, proposals, and follow-up decisions. Customer availability may require swapping this with a daytime personal call block.

Friday, 7:00–8:00: complete the weekly money and customer review. Send any promised customer update, then choose the single problem to work on next week.

At each shutdown, spend the final five minutes writing tomorrow's first action. Close the work. Use the remaining evening for your life and wind down for bed.

The weekend and the capacity limit

Saturday, 9:00–12:00: the week's main uninterrupted delivery or demonstration block.

Saturday, 1:00–3:00: scheduled owner visits or calls, prepare next week's prospect list, and finish a bounded customer task. Travel counts inside this time. If venue access is better at another hour, exchange blocks rather than adding hours.

Sunday, 5:00–6:00: review the upcoming calendar, select twenty new prospects for the four main outreach mornings, set customer commitments, and prepare Monday's first action. Keep the rest of Sunday free of planned business work.

This books nineteen business hours: five morning hours, six evening hours Monday through Thursday, one Friday hour, one hour of lunch calls, five Saturday hours, and one Sunday hour. Keep the twentieth hour as a buffer. If lunch calls are unavailable, place them in existing call blocks; do not automatically extend the workweek.

Suggested allocation within that same twenty-hour budget: eight hours selling and speaking with buyers; seven delivering and improving the paid offer; two managing money and planning; one deliberate learning; one relationship-building or a useful public progress update; and one buffer hour. These are categories for the existing blocks, not additional work.

The first weekly activity target is twenty researched first contacts and twenty appropriate follow-ups, with four to six substantive owner conversations as an initial aim. Review actual access and response rates. The target does not justify contacting irrelevant people or continuing after a clear no. If an active customer has an urgent contractual need, handle it and deliberately rebook the missed sales block.

A difficult-day fallback

If focus or an unexpected job demand disrupts the plan, check the most important customer obligation, spend fifteen minutes on one appropriate sales action, and prepare tomorrow's starting point. That preserves continuity. It does not erase existing delivery promises; communicate early if one may slip.

Use a visible timer, a single open task, and a phone outside reach for short work blocks. A weekly accountability conversation can help make progress observable. If the full schedule fails for two weeks, reduce it to twelve reliable hours and lengthen milestones. Avoid trying to repay missed work by sacrificing sleep.

Stage two: winning the first customer

Selling begins with understanding a costly problem. You do not need to win most conversations. You need a fitting buyer, a credible offer, and a repeatable way to reach enough of those buyers.

A simple opening: “I'm speaking with venue owners about what happens to private-event inquiries after the first response. Could I ask how you handle that today?” Use that wording only when it accurately describes what you are doing.

Keep a first discovery call to about twenty minutes. Spend the first few minutes understanding the business, most of the middle on a recent example, and the end on whether a next step is useful.

Ask:

1. “Walk me through the last time this happened.”
2. “How often does that happen?”
3. “What does it cost in staff time, missed opportunities, or contribution?”
4. “What have you already tried, and what still goes wrong?”
5. “Who would approve a small paid test, and what would make the test worthwhile?”

Repeat back your understanding and ask whether you got it right. Distinguish lost sales from lost contribution: a $2,000 booking is not necessarily $2,000 of profit. Use the owner's figures where available. Mark estimates as estimates and avoid claiming that every recovered-looking sale would otherwise have been lost.

When there is a fit, explain the proposed deliverables and ask for a decision: “I can set up the agreed workflow for one location, train your manager, and report the results. The pilot is [price], starts [date], and covers [scope]. Would you like to move forward?” Replace the placeholders with real terms. Say the price calmly and give the owner time to respond.

If they say it is too expensive, ask whether the issue is available budget, uncertain value, timing, or a comparison with another option. Address the actual issue. A smaller scope may make sense; an automatic discount may not. If there is no fit, leave the conversation respectfully.

If they need to think, ask what remains unresolved and whether they want to choose a follow-up date. If they decline or ask you to stop, close the opportunity. For nonresponses, a limited cadence such as two, five, and ten business days can be tested when appropriate, then close the loop.

After each call, spend five minutes noting: what the buyer actually said, one question you missed, the reason for the decision, and the next step. Record conversations only with permission. Practice the single most common weakness in the next morning's ten-minute block.

Funnel arithmetic only: one hundred well-selected contacts might produce ten qualified conversations; a twenty-percent close rate on those conversations would produce two customers. Those conversion rates are assumptions, not your expected results. Track each step separately. Poor contact response, weak qualification, and weak closing need different fixes.

Paul Graham's early-startup advice emphasizes finding users directly and personally helping them get value. That supports doing the first installations and customer conversations yourself. [Do Things That Don't Scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)

Stage three: delivering, renewing, and reaching several customers

In days fifteen through thirty, prioritize delivery of the first paid pilot and continued sales activity. In months two and three, aim to learn whether several customers buy substantially the same thing and whether early customers renew. These are review windows, not an income forecast.

At onboarding, agree on the starting measurement, the client's required actions, the work you control, and what a useful result looks like. Give the client an early visible sign that implementation is progressing. Do not promise a revenue outcome you cannot control.

Use a repeatable order: gather authorized access, establish the baseline, configure the workflow, check it with sample cases, train the responsible person, launch the agreed scope, and review the records. Document what actually worked for the next customer.

Send one concise weekly update: completed work, observed results, unresolved issues, and the client's next action. For Regulars, keep your planned monthly owner review and agreed records. Without reliable records, report the limits of what you can attribute to the program.

Ask for renewal using actual evidence. A customer who started on day twenty-five does not have a full month of results at the end of your first calendar month. Use that customer's actual renewal date.

After a verified useful result, ask whether the owner knows another similar owner who might benefit. Ask permission to create a short case study covering the starting problem, your intervention, the measured change, the timeframe, and the limits. Never invent testimonials or borrow another company's results.

The work-ethic shift here is reliability. Keep selling, but limit concurrent onboarding to what measured hours support. Refuse or separately price additions outside the agreed scope. If every sale requires a new custom product, reconsider what you are offering before multiplying customers.

Stage four: replacing employment income

Reaching the revenue target can take months or longer, and the chosen offer may fail. A first-month windfall is not a dependable basis for resignation.

My proposed departure gate is:

1. Cleared, earned business cash can support your actual personal baseline after expenses, an appropriate tax reserve, and business reserves for three to six consecutive months.
2. You have roughly six months of essential personal expenses available separately, plus sufficient business cash for commitments. Six months at $6,000–$8,000 is $36,000–$48,000; your essential-expense figure may be lower than your desired spending.
3. Your post-employment budget includes health coverage, retirement saving, and unpaid time off where those are currently supported by employment.
4. Losing the largest client would not immediately force missed personal bills or failed customer delivery.
5. You can show how new customers have been won beyond a single lucky introduction, and some customers have renewed.
6. The delivery workload is sustainable, with paid help included in the economics wherever needed.

These are conservative decision rules for this plan, not guarantees or universal requirements. If the business has proven demand but time is the constraint, a mutually agreed reduction in employment hours can be an intermediate option where available. Do not assume your employer offers it.

Stage five: full-time owner and a small team

Your new weekday can run from about 8:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m., after a morning routine suited to your sleep and life. At 8:00, review cash, customer commitments, and the main constraint for thirty minutes. From 8:30 to 10:30, sell or speak with buyers. From 10:30 to noon, deliver or coach delivery. Take lunch. From 1:00 to 3:00, improve the recurring process or product. From 3:00 to 4:00, handle customer reviews, hiring, or partnerships. Use 4:00 to 5:00 for administration, unexpected issues, and tomorrow's plan. Change the allocation when actual evidence identifies a different constraint.

Your weekly pattern should include a Monday commitment meeting, protected selling time, a midweek review of customer quality, and a Friday cash and retention review. Keep some direct customer contact after you have a team.

Hire against a repeated task with a clear standard. A possible trigger is a task taking more than five founder hours weekly for several weeks while paid demand exceeds delivery capacity. That number is a planning prompt. The real gate is sufficient margin and cash to pay the person, a defined result, and a way to inspect quality.

Start by writing a one-page role: the result needed, hours or capacity, relevant skills, pay, who decides what, and what successful first-month work looks like. Ask trusted former colleagues, Morehouse and BU contacts, and relevant operator communities for candidates. Interview for evidence of similar work. Use a small paid trial with a defined output and inspect accuracy, reliability, communication, and judgment.

Teach the task in stages: demonstrate it, do it together, watch them do it, then review completed work against a checklist. Start with one client or small scope. Include the full cost in your model. Contractor or employee status and written agreements should fit the actual working arrangement.

A cofounder is a different decision from buying help. Explore working together on a bounded project, discuss commitment and complementary responsibilities, and get ownership, decision rights, vesting, and work ownership documented with qualified help before treating the partnership as settled. YC offers a free place to meet potential cofounders; meeting a candidate does not establish fit. [YC Co-Founder Matching](https://www.ycombinator.com/cofounder-matching)

The work-ethic change is maintaining standards through other people. Your personal output matters, but so does whether the team can deliver without constant rescue.

Stage six: mid-level growth and a company that can last

At, for example, $30,000–$100,000 in monthly company revenue, the appropriate structure will differ greatly between a service business and software. Treat the range as an illustration of a growing company, not a forecast or an automatic hiring threshold.

Keep a clear customer segment and one or two acquisition methods that produce profitable customers. Document the sales explanation, onboarding, delivery, customer support, billing, and renewal process. Test a partner channel only where the partner already serves the same customer and incentives are clear.

Review the money left after delivery and replacement labor, customer retention, acquisition spending, time to recover acquisition costs, customer concentration, and staff workload. Explain each in plain terms to the team. New sales do not repair a service that existing customers consistently leave.

Assign one responsible person for each recurring outcome and give them enough authority to achieve it. Set objectives at the start of the week, ask about obstacles midweek, and review results at the end. Give specific feedback privately, recognize useful work, and address performance problems while they are still small.

Later, test whether operations can continue without you: first for one day, then several days, and eventually two weeks. Document what breaks and improve it. Maintain clean financial records, clear ownership of assets, dependable customer agreements, and leadership coverage. These make future ownership choices more practical; they do not guarantee an exit or valuation.

Your venture studio earns more attention when one business has dependable customer value, cash, delivery capacity, and someone accountable for daily operations. Give any new venture a fixed budget, one owner, a narrow test, and a stop date. Expanding the number of businesses also expands the work of management.

Skill development in the order you need it

For the first month, emphasize understanding buyers, explaining a concrete offer, asking for a paid decision, delivering the promised work, and understanding cash. Develop hiring, delegation, channel partnerships, and management as actual demand creates a need.

Limit structured study to about one hour of the weekly plan initially. Course completion can extend beyond the suggested week. Your learning output is an improved action, not a pile of certificates. Ask for human feedback on real calls and proposals when possible; an AI role-play is practice, not market validation.

Being easier to trust and work with

Before a conversation, learn enough about the person and business to ask a relevant question. Arrive on time and state how long you need. Put the phone away. Listen through the full answer and pause before responding. Summarize the problem and invite correction.

Explain what you can do in ordinary language. If you do not know an answer, say so and give a realistic time to check. When you make a mistake, identify it, propose the repair, and follow through. Use a written promise list so a small commitment does not disappear.

When someone challenges your idea, ask what experience led them to that concern. You can disagree after understanding the objection. A customer can decline and still leave with a good impression of you.

Make requests small and specific: a fifteen-minute introduction, feedback on one offer, or a defined paid task. Show progress before requesting a larger commitment. A teammate needs fair compensation or clearly documented ownership, useful responsibility, and a dependable working relationship. A customer needs value. A mentor needs a focused question and evidence that you used prior advice.

Set a weekly relationship habit: two thoughtful reconnections or introduction requests, one useful contribution where you can help, and one update to someone who has already helped. These fit the existing time allocation. Keep public building to a small, truthful progress update until it demonstrably brings qualified buyers.

Handling discouraging thoughts

Use three sentences: the thought, the facts, and the next action.

Example: “I'm bad at sales.” Facts: “I have had five calls, I have not asked clear budget questions, and I have not yet reviewed a call with anyone experienced.” Action: “Practice that question for ten minutes and use it on the next relevant call.”

Example: “They ignored me because my idea is bad.” Facts: “I do not know whether the right person saw the message.” Action: “Make one appropriate follow-up or try a better introduction, then move on if there is no interest.”

Example: “I'm behind, so I need to launch everything.” Facts: “My available work time is limited.” Action: “Give today's block to the offer with the strongest paid evidence.”

Let confidence develop from accurate promises and completed actions. Useful optimism means believing you can learn and improve while staying honest about the current numbers. Your worth is not a weekly sales total.
