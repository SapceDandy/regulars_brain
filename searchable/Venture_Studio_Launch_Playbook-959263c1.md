Venture Studio Launch Playbook

Regulars KUDJO and a remote venue service

Prepared for Devon Dudley  |  Version 1  |  September 14 2026

Start with paid customer work that can fund the products. Keep Regulars and KUDJO as the studio’s two core concepts. Test a third, remote venue service only where owner interviews show an urgent, valuable problem. Give the strongest paid offer most of your time.

The first 30 days should establish whether customers will pay, whether delivery creates value, and how much cash remains for you. A $6,000–$10,000 monthly personal income within 30 days is a stretch objective, not a dependable forecast. No checklist guarantees a successful company or exit.



Planned sequence. A product moves to a live pilot only after payment, scope, data access, and any venue-specific operating requirements are settled.

Starting assumptions

You begin solo in Texas and retain your job while validating. Use 20 hours a week as the initial capacity plan; a 40-hour alternative appears later. Current customers, available cash, existing entities, and whether your income target is before or after tax still need answers.

Your prior KUDJO notes describe a built codebase; the July 2 snapshot had fee recording but no completed merchant charging. Check the current version first. Regulars Core keeps the locker requirement; Connect, cameras, and hands-free presence detection come after the core program works.



The income target

Monthly recurring revenue is only the starting number. Pay yourself from cleared, earned cash after delivery costs, business bills, taxes, refunds, and a reserve. Customer membership receipts handled for a venue are not studio revenue.

Set up the company

Do these once, reusing existing assets where appropriate. Filing is a dependency for the proposed LLC’s EIN and banking; customer interviews can run while approval is pending.

01  Inventory what already exists. List entities, EINs, bank accounts, domains, code repositories, collaborators, signed customer agreements, and amounts actually collected. Review your employment agreement for outside work and invention ownership; use your own accounts and equipment.

Done when: One page separates existing assets, proposed ideas, and unresolved ownership.

02  Choose the entity path. If you have no suitable entity and no near-term institutional round, take a single Texas operating LLC as the starting proposal to a Texas business attorney or CPA. Review existing KUDJO ownership before moving anything. Ask whether Regulars needs a separate operating entity before live venue work.

Done when: You know the legal party that will own the new work and sign the first agreement.

Brands and assumed names do not create separate liability protection. A studio holding company and product subsidiaries may become appropriate; forming three companies immediately also creates extra administration. If an institutional round is imminent, discuss the corporate structure and tax consequences before filing or transferring IP.

03  Choose a name and registered agent. Check the Texas entity name, relevant trademark conflicts, domain, and social handles. Select an eligible Texas registered agent and obtain consent. Use a lawful service address suited to a publicly documented business; a mailbox alone does not replace a registered office.

Done when: The name, agent, address, ownership, and management choice are ready.

04  File the formation. Submit Texas Certificate of Formation Form 205 through the Secretary of State process. The state filing fee is $300, excluding payment surcharges and optional services. Save the accepted formation record.

Done when: The state has accepted the entity, not merely received an application. Texas LLC formation form and $300 filing fee

05  Apply for the EIN. After formation, use the IRS application directly. It is free. Enter the responsible party information yourself and save the confirmation letter privately.

Done when: The EIN confirmation is saved and matches the entity name. IRS free EIN application and formation sequence

06  Document the brands and ownership. Prepare the operating agreement and assignments for work the company should own. Have every contractor’s signed agreement address IP and confidentiality before work begins. File applicable assumed names; the Texas state fee is $25 per certificate.

Done when: You can show who owns each product, domain, and reusable studio component. Texas assumed name instructions and $25 filing fee

Sell before expanding the build

Use one prospect list across the studio. Pitch the problem that matters to that owner; introduce another brand only when the first engagement reveals a fit.

10  Build the first 30 account list. List 10 owner-operated venues with lockers or funded locker plans, 10 food businesses with repeat customers, and 10 event-capable venues with a private-event inquiry channel. Overlap is fine. Pick one nearby territory for visits and sell the event service remotely.

Done when: Each record has venue, owner or manager, contact source, problem hypothesis, and next action.

11  Request evidence calls. Aim for 10 substantive owner conversations in week one. Ask for a 15-minute review of how the problem works today. Start with warm relationships. Use truthful one-to-one outreach and respect opt-outs; do not cold-text consumers or upload purchased numbers.

Done when: Each conversation records current behavior, cost, decision maker, and urgency.

12  Ask the same five questions. Ask: What happened the last time? How often? What did it cost in time or contribution? What do you use now? Who could approve a small paid pilot this week? Ask to see a redacted record where practical.

Done when: You can explain the owner’s problem in their terms, using a real example.

13  Present a bounded offer. Use the starting prices on the next three pages. Show the deliverables, one location, volume limit, responsibilities, start date, acceptance check, cancellation and refund terms, and what is excluded. These prices are hypotheses to test.

Done when: The owner can accept a specific scope without needing a broad custom proposal.

14  Ask for a paid commitment. Send the scoped agreement and invoice the same day after a qualified conversation. Do not treat a compliment, unsigned letter of intent, or promised future introduction as a sale. Keep deposits available for the agreed delivery and refund obligations.

Done when: The agreement is signed and a payment has cleared.

15  Choose the active priority. After the first 10 qualified conversations, give the offer with the clearest pain, paid interest, and manageable delivery 70% of operating time. Regulars and KUDJO remain core concepts, but launch at most one new pilot at a time at 20 hours a week.

Done when: One offer owns the next sales and delivery block on your calendar.

16  Repeat a small daily cadence. On four workdays each week, make five researched first contacts and five appropriate follow-ups. Use two booked call blocks and one delivery block. Follow up after about 2, 5, and 10 business days unless asked to stop.

Done when: The list shows the next step for every live opportunity.

Funnel arithmetic, not a benchmark: 100 contacted accounts × 20% reaching a qualified conversation × 25% buying = 5 clients. If conversations stall, change access or targeting; if qualified owners decline, inspect value, trust, scope, and price before building more features.



Regulars launch instructions

Buyer: an independent bar owner with an existing locker program or a funded, feasible installation. Starting offer to test: $2,500 setup plus $499 per location per month for a managed Core pilot. Hardware, installation, alcohol, and venue staffing are separate venue costs.

This is a proposed launch package. Your earlier 10% membership-fee model remains an alternative to test after collection and membership economics are clear; do not silently add both charges. Begin with a transparent fixed software and service fee.

17  Confirm the physical prerequisite. Ask for locker photos, capacity, ownership, installation timing, and the staff member who will operate the program. For the fastest pilot, use an existing program. If new lockers are required, obtain a venue-approved quote and delivery date.

Done when: The venue can actually provide the lockers without studio inventory spending.

18  Resolve the operating rules. Give TABC or qualified Texas alcohol counsel the venue’s permit type and exact written flow: bottle purchase, custody, labeling, access, service, membership benefits, unused balances, seat priority, and tips or service charges. Ask the venue to confirm installation and insurance requirements.

Done when: The venue has an actionable, documented basis to operate this specific program. Public FAQs alone do not approve the concept. TABC licensing and operating questions

19  Write the membership offer. With the owner, set available slots, fee, included benefits, service hours, seat rules, cancellation, and refund policy. Separate alcohol-related items for review. Ask the venue to invite a small founding group before increasing capacity.

Done when: The terms can be explained consistently by every staff member.

20  Build only the Core workflow. Use member ID, locker ID, bottle ID, check-in, service log, remaining balance or volume estimate, and member status. Demonstrate a scan or searchable pass. Keep the locker required; use the simplest reliable scan before wallet refinements.

Done when: Staff can enroll a member, locate the locker, record service, and correct a mistake.

21  Launch and train one venue. Collect setup payment against agreed milestones. Have the venue collect member payments directly. Train a manager and backup, run five sample member journeys, and launch a limited approved cohort.

Done when: The manager can operate a normal shift without you standing beside them.

22  Run the owner review. Log visits by day, paid memberships, renewals, service issues, and founder support time. Use your proposed monthly owner meeting to reconcile venue exports or an agreed manual log.

Done when: The owner sees contribution after perks, labor, fees, and hardware allocation, and decides whether to renew.

Pass condition: a paying venue, usable staff records, and customer value that supports renewal. Stop condition: no funded lockers, unresolved operating rules, or missing records. Do not count member prepayments as evidence of incremental profit; measure repeat visits and displacement of full-price purchases.



KUDJO launch instructions

Buyer: a restaurant or food truck with repeat-visit potential and an owner who will share redemption evidence. Start with one merchant and one food offer. Starting managed-service price to test: $750 setup plus $499 per location per month; merchant-funded discounts and agreed messaging or ad spending are separate.

Keep the original $0.20 plus 1% usage concept documented for later self-service. Confirm what the 1% applies to. If it applies to a $30 purchase, studio revenue is $0.50 per redemption; $10,000 of revenue would require 20,000 redemptions before costs. A managed launch fee changes the early economics.

23  Inspect the current product. Check the deployed version, onboarding, campaign creation, fee flags, merchant billing, and redemption tracking. Use the July code notes as a checklist, not proof of today’s state. Route early paid pilots through ordinary invoices if in-product charging is unfinished.

Done when: A merchant can onboard, run the selected offer, redeem it once, and receive an invoice.

24  Agree on the baseline. Collect four comparable weeks of visits or transactions if available, offer costs, typical order size, and food contribution. Select one goal, such as a second visit within 30 days.

Done when: The owner agrees on the comparison period and how campaign results will be logged.

25  Define one constrained offer. Start with an owner-approved food offer such as $5 off a $20 qualifying purchase. Set redemption quantity, expiration, day or time limits, exclusions, and total merchant exposure. AI negotiation, if used, must remain within those limits.

Done when: A test cannot exceed the merchant’s maximum authorized concession.

26  Choose a launch channel. Use an existing approved merchant email or messaging channel where consent and permissions fit the campaign. For new US business SMS through a local 10-digit number, complete brand and campaign registration and document opt-in and opt-out. Allow for approval delays.

Done when: The chosen channel is ready; a delayed SMS approval does not hold up a lawful QR or approved email pilot. Twilio US A2P 10DLC registration Twilio opt in and campaign approval requirements

27  Run the manual fallback. Give the merchant unique redemption codes or a scan flow, a daily log, and instructions for rejecting duplicates. Without POS access, reconcile code, date, purchase amount, and discount from an agreed register export or owner log.

Done when: Every claimed redemption has evidence, including failed and refunded redemptions.

28  Report contribution and renewal. Report redemptions, repeat visits, discount cost, messaging cost, managed fees, and estimated contribution. If volume permits, agree on a randomized holdout; otherwise label before-and-after results as directional and affected by seasonality.

Done when: The merchant chooses to renew, revise the offer, or stop based on economics.

Pass condition: a paid campaign with auditable records and credible contribution potential. Stop condition: the owner cannot measure redemptions, cannot authorize customer contact, or loses money on the agreed offer. Keep creator expansion and multi-POS integrations behind demonstrated demand.



Test the remote third business

Working name: Venue Follow Up Desk. Buyer: an event-capable restaurant, bar, brewery, or venue with meaningful inbound private-event inquiries. The service organizes inquiries, drafts or sends authorized follow-ups, and tracks quotes and bookings using the venue’s existing tools.

Starting price hypothesis: $1,500 setup plus $1,500 per month for one location, one inbox, up to 50 new event inquiries per month, and two scheduled review windows per business day. Exclude live phone coverage, event production, and unlimited revisions. Quote any expansion separately.

29  Audit the opportunity. Ask five owners for a redacted sample from their last 30 days of inquiries. Look for unanswered messages, stalled quotes, existing software, inquiry volume, and actual event contribution.

Done when: At least one owner has a visible loss or workload large enough to justify a paid test.

30  Prove the fee can make sense. Example only: three genuinely additional events at $750 contribution each create $2,250 before the $1,500 monthly fee. Include setup when evaluating initial payback. If the venue needs dozens of improbable extra bookings to break even, decline it.

Done when: The owner accepts a measurable value case without a promised booking count.

31  Sell execution around existing software. Compare the owner’s current tools with an existing event system such as Tripleseat. If the software already solves the issue and the owner runs it well, this service adds little. Sell setup and follow-through only where they are missing.

Done when: The paid scope identifies an actual operating gap. Tripleseat existing venue inquiry and event software

32  Configure one repeatable workflow. Connect an authorized shared inbox or use supervised exports. Create an inquiry register with date, event date, party size, budget, stage, owner, quote value, last contact, next action, and outcome. Use the venue’s price and availability sources.

Done when: Every incoming inquiry has one owner and a next action.

33  Control what can be sent. Begin with approved templates and human review. Confirm availability with the venue before committing. Do not let AI invent pricing, promise space, accept nonstandard terms, or handle card numbers. Stop follow-ups when a prospect declines or books.

Done when: The venue approves sample messages and the escalation rules before launch.

34  Reconcile weekly. Review response time during coverage hours, stalled inquiries, signed bookings, cancellations, collected deposits, and time spent. Distinguish a booked event from completed-event profit; establish a baseline before claiming recovery.

Done when: The owner renews because the service produces enough verified value.

Decision: keep this as a small service line inside the studio initially. Build a separate software product only after three paying clients need substantially the same workflow. Cap initial setup at eight founder hours and ongoing delivery at three hours per client per week; measure those assumptions. If no owner validates the problem, park it.

The scale mathematics

At $499 per location per month, 1,000 locations produce about $5.99 million in annual recurring revenue; 10,000 produce $59.88 million. At $1,000 per month, 10,000 locations produce $120 million. This illustrates the distribution and product value required; it is not a market-size estimate or valuation promise.

A hypothetical $100 million annual revenue business valued at 10 times revenue would be worth $1 billion. The multiple is an illustrative assumption, not a current benchmark or guaranteed outcome. Growth, retention, margins, market conditions, capital structure, and buyer demand matter.

Make each business possible to sell

Maintain clean ownership of code, domains, trademarks and content; signed contributor agreements; customer contracts with reviewed transfer provisions; separate brand financials; an explicit license for shared studio technology; data-use permissions; documented operations; and a record of revenue quality and churn. Avoid making a buyer dependent on undocumented personal access or work you cannot transfer.

At the 90-day review, set targets from actual baselines for renewal, customer concentration, cost to acquire, payback, support hours, and replacement labor. Separate subscription revenue from hardware, discounts, member receipts, and service projects.

Make the next decisions

Answer these in stages. The first group changes the immediate calendar and income model; the later groups become onboarding questions once a specific buyer is interested.

Before the next work session

2. How many studio hours can you reliably protect each week? Are you remaining at Home Depot during the launch?
